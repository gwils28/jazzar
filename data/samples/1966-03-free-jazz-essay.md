*Illustrative essay written for the Jazzar sample corpus.*

When Ornette Coleman's double quartet recorded *Free Jazz* for Atlantic in December 1960 — Coleman, Don Cherry, Scott LaFaro and Billy Higgins in one channel; Eric Dolphy, Freddie Hubbard, Charlie Haden and Ed Blackwell in the other — the thirty-seven-minute collective improvisation gave a name to a movement that had been gathering since Coleman's Los Angeles quartet reached New York in 1959.

Five years later, the "new thing" has an infrastructure. Cecil Taylor, whose *Unit Structures* for Blue Note is due this year, has built a pianism of clusters and velocity that owes as much to Bartók as to Bud Powell. Albert Ayler's Spiritual Unity trio with Gary Peacock and Sunny Murray has stripped the music down to vibrato and shriek. Archie Shepp's records for Impulse! have made the connection between the music and the civil-rights movement explicit. And in Chicago, Muhal Richard Abrams's Experimental Band has become the Association for the Advancement of Creative Musicians (AACM), founded in May 1965 as a cooperative for composers who found no place in the clubs.

The critical response has been split. The music's partisans hear a liberation from harmony and the chorus structure; its detractors hear a loss of craft. What is clear is that the center of gravity has moved. John Coltrane, the most prominent musician of the decade, has absorbed the new players rather than resisted them: his *Ascension*, recorded in June 1965 with eleven musicians including Shepp, Pharoah Sanders and Marion Brown, is the most widely heard free jazz recording to date, and his quartet's recent concerts have featured Sanders and drummer Rashied Ali alongside the regular members.

Whether the movement produces a repertoire, as bebop did, or remains a set of methods, the next five years will tell.
